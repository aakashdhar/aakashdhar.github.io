## 1 — Wed Aug 5 — Who Trains the Junior When the Agent Does the Grunt Work?

A couple of years ago I ran two intern cohorts, first-year developers, and the way they got good was completely predictable. You handed them the boring work, the small endpoints, the form validation, the fixes nobody fought over, and each week they got a little sharper without anyone formally teaching them.

Last month I went to hand a junior a first-year task and caught myself halfway through the sentence. The agent could produce all of it before he finished reading the ticket.

The grunt work was never valuable because it was hard. It was valuable because it was how you learned. You wrote the boring thing, got it a little wrong, watched it break, and the judgment settled in. The tedium was the tuition. AI is very good at exactly that bottom rung, and it is pulling the ladder up behind it.

So on your team, who is teaching the thing that used to be taught by doing, now that the doing is gone?

## 2 — Wed Aug 12 — When Building Is Cheap, Saying "Not Yet" Gets Expensive

One fast week, I let three things start. There was clearly capacity and no obvious reason to say wait.

All three worked. Two of them we then spent a fortnight quietly unwinding, not because they were badly built but because they should never have been built.

Here is what I learned the expensive way: building is nearly free now, and the cheaper starting got, the more expensive it became to start the wrong thing. The effort used to be its own filter. It said no on your behalf, quietly killing the half-formed ideas before they cost anything. Remove the effort and you remove the filter, and a working feature is far harder to kill than a proposal. It has momentum, a small constituency, a sunk cost everyone can see.

So the leverage moved to the front of the pipeline: guarding what gets started at all.

Now that building is cheap on your team, who is guarding what gets started, and is that person treated as doing real work, or as getting in the way?

## 3 — Wed Aug 19 — What Are Clients Paying For When They Can Watch the AI Work?

A client watched a demo come together in about an hour on a screen-share, then asked me, reasonably, why the rest of the project was going to take six weeks.

It was not a hostile question. It was the honest reaction of someone who had just seen a machine produce, in an hour, something that used to feel like real effort. And it forced a question I had been avoiding: when the client can see how fast the building goes now, what do they think they are paying me for?

For years, part of the value of delivery was that the effort was invisible and therefore easy to respect. AI did not just speed the work up. It made the speed visible, and stripped the effort of its old cover.

What survives is the judgment: knowing what to build, what "done" really means, catching the risk they did not know to ask about. That was always the real service. It was just easier to hide behind the hours.

When your client can see how fast the building has become, can you say, without flinching, what the rest of the money is for?

## 4 — Wed Aug 26 — You Can't Plan a Quarter When the Tools Change Every Month

I built a sensible quarterly plan in April. By the end of the month a chunk of it was quietly obsolete, not because we were wrong about the goal but because the tooling had moved. Two of the hard problems had become roughly trivial. One of the easy ones had turned into a question mark.

I have made a lot of plans. I had never watched one go stale that fast, for that reason.

Planning has always been a bet against uncertainty, but the uncertainty used to sit in familiar places. What is new is uncertainty about your own capability. You genuinely do not know what your team will be able to do cheaply in eight weeks, because the tools that decide it keep changing.

So I have stopped planning harder. I plan the direction firmly and the route loosely. The goal I commit to. The path I hold open on purpose, because locking it in early buys a precision I cannot honour.

Are your plans still a source of confidence, or have they quietly become a source of drag?

## 5 — Wed Sep 2 — The Technical Debt You Can't See in a Fast Team

We had a genuinely great quarter by every number I report on. Throughput up, features shipped, client happy. And I had a low, persistent unease the whole time.

Because a fast team leaves a particular kind of mess, and none of it shows up in the numbers that made the quarter look good. Not one big shortcut you can point to, but a thousand tiny defaults nobody consciously chose. The agent made a sensible local call, the developer accepted it because it worked, the feature shipped because it passed. Multiply that across a fast quarter.

I found ours when a change that should have taken an hour took most of a day, because it touched four decisions nobody remembered making, and each one fought back. That is the tell: the debt is invisible until you try to change something near it, and then it charges you all at once.

Someone has to go looking for the cost while the chart is still green, which is exactly when nobody wants to hear about it.

Do you actually know what your fast weeks are leaving behind, or will you find out the first time you try to change something near it?

## 6 — Mon Jul 6 — When AI Does the Satisfying Part, What's Left to Enjoy?

A developer who genuinely loves to build told me recently that the work had started to feel a little hollow. I understood before he finished the sentence.

I spent eight and a half years as a backend developer before I moved into delivery, so I am not saying this from the outside. The pleasure was never the feature existing. It was the building, the quiet, absorbed hours of making something work with your own hands. Hand the making to a machine and you keep the output and lose the flow, and the output was never the part that fed anyone.

We talk a lot about what AI does to productivity and almost nothing about what it does to satisfaction. Those are not the same axis. A tool can make a team faster and, in the same motion, quietly remove the thing that made the work feel worth doing. And that cost shows up late, as people drifting, long after the chart said everything was healthy.

When the machine does the satisfying part, what are you doing to keep the work feeling worth doing, for the people who used to love it most?

## 7 — Wed Sep 16 — What Should a PM Learn Now That the Busywork Is Gone?

I spent years getting good at things that are now free. The clean status update, the well-kept board, the estimate stitched from context, the report reshaped for each audience. I was proud of those skills, and the tools made most of them table stakes almost overnight.

So I have had to actually answer the question, not avoid it: if the things I got good at are now the things a machine does for free, what do I bet the next five years on?

The pattern in what survived is worth noticing. Everything left standing is a judgment skill, not a production skill. Knowing whether a green status is true. Deciding what to build, and harder, what to refuse to build. Being the name attached to an outcome when something an agent produced goes wrong.

The busywork was not only work. It was cover. On a shaky week you could still produce an immaculate report and look competent. That option is gone. What is left is whether you were right, and you cannot fake that with formatting.

Now that the busywork is gone and the cover with it, what are you actually betting on getting better at?
